AI writing tools fall into a few broad groups, and most disappointment comes from picking a tool built for a different job. This guide walks through the questions to answer before you commit.

## Start with the job, not the tool

Write down the three writing tasks you do most often. They usually fall into one of these groups:

- **Correcting and polishing** — spelling, grammar, punctuation, clarity. Grammar-focused assistants are built for this.
- **Rewriting** — paraphrasing, simplifying or changing tone of text you already have.
- **Generating** — producing first drafts of articles, product descriptions or ads from a prompt.
- **Summarising** — condensing long documents into key points.

A tool that excels at generating long articles may be clumsy as an everyday proofreader, and the reverse is also true.

## Test with your own material

Marketing pages show the best examples. Instead, run every tool you are considering on the same sample:

1. A paragraph of your own writing with a few deliberate mistakes.
2. A short brief for something you would genuinely publish.
3. A long document you need summarised.

Compare the results side by side. Count how many changes you would have to make before you could use the output. That number is a better guide than any feature list.

## Check facts — every time

Generative AI can produce confident but wrong statements. If a tool drafts content for you, check every claim, number, name and date against a reliable source before publishing. This matters most for health, money and legal topics.

## Understand how your text is used

Before pasting client work, unpublished manuscripts or company documents into any online tool, read:

- the privacy policy and terms of service;
- whether your text may be used to train models, and whether you can opt out;
- whether business plans offer extra data controls.

If you work for an organisation, check its AI policy first.

## Work out the real cost

Free plans are useful for testing but often limit words, features or daily uses. When comparing paid plans, check:

- whether limits are per day, per month or per credit;
- whether the advertised price requires annual billing;
- how many seats you need;
- what happens to your saved work if you downgrade.

Our [free vs freemium vs paid guide](/guides/free-vs-freemium-vs-paid-software/) explains these pricing labels in more detail.

## A simple decision checklist

- Does it do the specific job you need most often?
- Did it perform well on **your** sample, not just the demo?
- Are its privacy terms acceptable for the material you will use?
- Do the free plan limits let you test properly?
- Does the paid plan's cost make sense for your volume?
